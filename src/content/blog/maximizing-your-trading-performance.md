When a trader wants better results, the instinct is almost always to look for a better entry. A new indicator, a refined pattern, a different timeframe.

It is rarely where the improvement is. A trader with a mediocre edge and excellent execution will comfortably outperform a trader with a good edge and inconsistent execution. The second trader usually has no idea, because they are measuring the strategy rather than themselves.

Here is where the gains actually tend to be.

## 1. Position sizing does more than entry selection

Two traders can take identical trades for a month and end with completely different results, purely from how they sized.

The common failure is sizing by conviction — larger when a setup "looks good", smaller when uncertain. This feels rational and is quietly destructive, because conviction is not correlated with outcome nearly as strongly as it feels. It guarantees your largest positions sit on your most emotionally-charged trades.

Fixed fractional risk — the same small percentage on every trade — removes the problem entirely. It also makes your results interpretable: when every trade risks the same, the equity curve tells you about the strategy rather than about your moods.

If you want to vary size, vary it by a measured input such as volatility, not by feel. Sizing off something like ATR keeps your currency risk stable when the market's range changes, instead of accidentally doubling your exposure the week gold starts moving.

## 2. Trade fewer sessions, better

Most instruments do not behave the same way all day. Gold and the major pairs have hours where liquidity is deep and moves follow through, and hours where price chops in a narrow range and stops out everyone on both sides.

Trading through the dead hours costs you twice: the direct losses, and the concentration you no longer have when conditions improve.

Reviewing your own results by session is one of the highest-yield hours you can spend. Most traders discover their profit is concentrated in a specific window, and that a different window is quietly funding the losses. Cutting the second one requires no new skill.

## 3. Review with data, not memory

Memory is an unreliable narrator. It over-weights recent trades, dramatic trades and painful trades, and quietly deletes the boring ones that make up most of the record.

A journal fixes this, provided it captures enough to be analysed later:

- Entry, exit, size, and the currency result
- The setup name, from your written definitions
- The reason for entry, written *before* the outcome was known
- A screenshot of the chart at entry
- Your state — rushed, confident, recovering from a loss

After fifty trades, sort by setup. Sort by session. Sort by your state field. The patterns that emerge are usually not the ones you expected, and each one is a change you can make without learning anything new.

## 4. Build a routine that makes execution boring

Consistent output comes from consistent input. Traders who perform steadily tend to run something close to the same sequence every day:

- **Before:** review the economic calendar for scheduled releases, mark levels, note the day's plan and the maximum loss that ends the session.
- **During:** take only setups that meet the written definition. Log each one as it happens, not from memory afterwards.
- **After:** grade the decisions, not the results. Note anything that broke the plan.

The goal is not to make trading exciting. It is to make it repeatable enough that your results reflect your strategy rather than your day.

## What to stop doing

As useful as the additions:

- **Stop changing strategy after a losing week.** Every strategy has losing weeks. Changing after each one means never trading any strategy long enough to know if it works.
- **Stop adding indicators to explain losses.** A loss usually is not a missing signal; it is a normal part of the distribution.
- **Stop trading to recover.** The recovery trade is reliably the worst trade of the week.
- **Stop measuring in percentage returns alone.** Track maximum drawdown too. A 20% gain with a 25% drawdown is a worse result than 8% with 4%, and only one of them is survivable at scale.

## The compounding part

None of these are dramatic. Sizing consistently, dropping a bad session, reviewing honestly, keeping a routine — each is worth a few percent.

But they compound, and unlike a new entry signal they do not stop working when conditions change. Performance improvement is mostly the removal of avoidable losses, and avoidable losses are found in process, not in setups.
