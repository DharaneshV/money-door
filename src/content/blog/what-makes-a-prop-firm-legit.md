Prop firms have made real capital accessible to traders who could never have funded a serious account themselves. They have also attracted a large number of operators whose actual business model is selling evaluation fees to people who will not pass.

Both types market identically. Telling them apart takes a handful of specific checks — none of which involve the size of the promised account.

## Start with the payouts

Everything else is secondary to one question: does this firm actually pay?

- **Look for payouts with proof, not screenshots.** Any image can be fabricated. Look for a consistent, dated public record and for traders discussing payouts in places the firm does not control.
- **Check the payout schedule and whether it is honoured.** A stated 14-day cycle means nothing if the community reports it running to 60.
- **Read the payout conditions.** Some firms attach requirements — minimum trading days, consistency thresholds, KYC steps — that are only discovered at withdrawal time.
- **Look for how they handle disputes.** Every firm has denied a payout at some point. What matters is whether the reasoning was a published rule or an after-the-fact interpretation.

A firm with a long, boring, verifiable payout history is worth more than one with an aggressive affiliate programme and a bigger headline account.

## Read the rules before you pay

The rulebook is the product. If it is vague, that vagueness is not an accident — it is discretion the firm can exercise against you later.

Specifically, you want unambiguous answers on:

- Daily and overall drawdown: the exact percentages, and whether they measure **balance or equity**
- Whether the overall limit is **static or trailing**, and when trailing stops
- The daily reset time, in a stated timezone
- Consistency rules and how they are calculated
- News, weekend and holding restrictions
- What happens to your account and fee if you breach
- Whether the profit split changes after scaling

If any of these takes more than a few minutes to find, treat that as the answer.

## Understand what you are actually trading

Most retail prop accounts are simulated. That is not automatically a problem — but it changes what the firm's incentives are, and you should know which model you are in.

- **Simulated/demo accounts:** the firm pays winning traders from its own revenue. This works when enough traders fail to fund the ones who succeed. It becomes fragile if too many succeed at once.
- **Live-routed accounts:** trades reach a real broker and real liquidity. Rarer, generally slower to scale, and structurally more durable.

Ask which broker and liquidity provider sits behind the platform. A firm that will not say is telling you something.

## Check the operating substance

- **A registered legal entity, with a jurisdiction you can look up.** Prop firms are generally not regulated as brokers, so "unregulated" is not itself disqualifying — but an unidentifiable company is.
- **A trading platform you recognise**, not a bespoke web terminal with no order history export.
- **Real support.** Send a specific rules question before paying anything. The speed and precision of the reply is a reliable signal.
- **Track record.** A firm that has operated through a volatile market has been stress-tested. One founded eight months ago has not.

## The warning signs

Individually these are yellow flags; together they are a pattern:

- Guaranteed or "risk-free" funding language
- Discounts that are permanently about to expire
- Profit targets that are trivially easy relative to the drawdown allowed
- Rules that change without notice or version history
- A community presence made up almost entirely of affiliates
- No published address, entity name or leadership
- Pressure to buy a larger account immediately after a failure

## The check most traders skip

Before paying any evaluation fee, work out whether the rules are compatible with how you actually trade.

A firm can be entirely legitimate and still be wrong for you. If you trade breakouts around economic releases and the firm forbids news trading, you will fail — not because you were cheated, but because you bought a product that prohibits your strategy. If you hold positions over several days and the overall drawdown is equity-based and trailing, your normal open floating loss may breach a rule before your idea has had time to work.

Read the rulebook as a specification for the trading you intend to do. If they do not match, the correct decision is to walk away — regardless of how legitimate the firm is.
