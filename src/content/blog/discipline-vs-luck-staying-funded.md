Passing an evaluation and keeping a funded account are two different achievements, and the first one can be done on luck.

Take enough oversized positions in a trending market and some traders will hit a 10% target in four days. The account is real, the payout might even be real. But nothing repeatable happened — and the same behaviour that produced the pass will, given enough sessions, produce the breach.

The traders still funded a year later are rarely the ones who passed fastest.

## Luck and skill produce identical short-term results

This is the uncomfortable part. Over a small number of trades, a good process and a lucky streak look exactly the same from the inside. Both feel like confidence. Both produce green days.

The difference only shows up over sample size — which is precisely the period a funded account exposes you to. An evaluation might last two weeks. A funded account has no end date. Whatever edge you actually have, real or imagined, gets measured eventually.

So the useful question after a winning week is not "how much did I make?" It is "would this have made money if the market had done something else?"

## Process over outcome

A trade can be well-executed and lose. A trade can be reckless and win. If you judge your decisions by their results, you will systematically learn the wrong lessons from both.

The practical fix is to grade the decision separately from the outcome:

- Did the setup meet my criteria before I entered — or did I adjust the criteria to fit a trade I wanted?
- Was the position sized by my rule, or by how confident I felt?
- Was the stop placed at my invalidation point, or at a distance that felt tolerable?
- Did I close for a planned reason, or an emotional one?

A losing trade that scores well on all four is a good trade. A winning trade that fails three of them is a warning, and treating it as a success is how a strategy quietly stops being a strategy.

## What discipline actually looks like

Discipline is a poor word for it, because it suggests willpower — gritting your teeth and resisting a bad trade. Willpower runs out, particularly after losses, which is exactly when you need it.

What works better is removing the decision from the moment:

- **Predefined risk per trade.** Not "small" — a number, applied before you see the chart.
- **A daily stop.** A currency amount at which you are finished for the day, decided in advance. This single rule prevents most account-ending sessions.
- **A written setup definition.** If you cannot describe your entry criteria in a sentence someone else could apply, you do not have criteria — you have impressions.
- **A fixed routine.** Same preparation, same session, same review. Routine is what makes execution boring, and boring execution is the goal.
- **A journal.** Entry, exit, reason, screenshot, and how you felt. The last field is the one that reveals the pattern.

None of this is sophisticated. That is why it works — sophisticated rules get abandoned under pressure.

## Where accounts are actually lost

Very few funded accounts die from one catastrophic idea. Most die in a recognisable sequence:

1. A normal loss, or a run of them.
2. The trader increases size to recover it faster.
3. A setup is taken that does not meet criteria, because waiting feels intolerable.
4. That loss is larger than the first, because size was increased.
5. Steps 2 to 4 repeat, compressed into a single session.

Every step is emotionally reasonable. Together they breach a drawdown limit in an afternoon.

The daily stop exists specifically to interrupt this sequence at step two. It is the cheapest rule in trading and the most frequently overridden.

## Consistency is the actual product

Prop firms do not want traders who make 10% in a week. That trader is expensive and unpredictable. They want traders who make modest returns, repeatedly, inside defined risk — because those traders can be scaled.

This is worth internalising, because it means the behaviour that keeps a funded account is also the behaviour that grows it. There is no separate aggressive phase to graduate into.

Luck can pass an evaluation. Only a process survives one.
